# Talking with God: Gratitude, Lament, Petition and Intercession

**Research date:** 29 October 2026  
**Primary teachers/resources:** John Mark Comer, Practicing the Way, and its published *Prayer Practice*.  
**Status:** Independent, source-linked synthesis for FaithHub; not an official Practicing the Way guide. Prompts, examples and prayers here are original, not transcriptions.

## Executive finding: distinguish the two sets of “four”

Practicing the Way names **four broad movements of prayer**: (1) talking **to** God (often through received words such as the Lord’s Prayer), (2) talking **with** God (honest conversation), (3) listening **to** God, and (4) being **with** God (contemplative presence). These are the **four sessions** of its Prayer Practice, not the four terms in this document.

Inside **talking with God**, the official Session 02 and the publisher-authorised Bible.com sample group forms of personal prayer under **three headings**: gratitude, lament, and petition **and** intercession. We treat petition and intercession as separate, related practices so that each can have a clear prompt. This is an application of the material, **not a claim** that John Mark Comer created a mandatory four-step prayer method.

**Foundational passages:** Luke 11:1–13 (teaching and asking), Matthew 6:9–13 (the Lord’s Prayer), Philippians 4:6–7 (thanksgiving and asking), Psalms 13 and 79 (lament), 1 Timothy 2:1–2 (prayer for others), 1 Thessalonians 5:16–18 (thanksgiving).

## 1. Thankfulness / gratitude — bring what is good

**Meaning.** Speak to the Father about gifts, provision, joy, beauty, relationships and mercy, including very ordinary goodness. In the official sample, gratitude is more than mood improvement: it answers the prior gift of God in Christ and the Holy Spirit. This is why “thankfulness” is not just a psychological technique.

**Biblical anchors.** Psalm 103:1–5; James 1:17; 1 Thessalonians 5:16–18; Philippians 4:6; John 3:16.

**Notice:** What good thing did I experience? Who was involved? What did I receive that I did not earn? Can I thank God for one thing without pretending everything is good?

**Original sample prayer:** “Father, thank you for the good that was here today, even in ordinary moments. Help me receive it rather than hurry past it.”

**Practice (1–2 minutes):** Recall one *specific* moment, perhaps a sound, meal, kindness or moment of help. Let it register, then say thank you. If finding gratitude is difficult, don't force it. You can begin in lament instead.

**Cautions:** Gratitude does not oblige a person to reinterpret harm as good, minimise grief, deny injustice or suppress anger. It is also not a competition to name three things.

## 2. Lament — bring what is painful, wrong or unanswered

**Meaning.** Speak honestly to God about grief, fear, confusion, evil, disappointment and delayed answers. Biblical lament is addressed *to God*: it keeps relationship open, even when language is raw. Comer’s archived 2017 teaching explicitly introduces lament in the tension between promised prayer and unanswered prayer.

**Biblical anchors.** Psalm 13; Psalm 10; Psalm 79; Psalm 88 (which does not resolve neatly); Lamentations 3:19–26; Mark 14:32–36; Romans 8:26.

**Notice:** What feels heavy or unjust? Where do I feel that—in my chest, jaw, stomach, shoulders or breath? What am I afraid to tell God? Where is the unanswered prayer?

**Original sample prayer:** “Lord, this hurts and I don’t understand. I am angry and tired. Please hear me. I need you here, even without an answer tonight.”

**Practice (2–5 minutes):** Read a lament Psalm, perhaps Psalm 13 or 88. Name the situation and feeling without editing them into a respectable performance. If helpful, write a short letter beginning “God, I don't know what to do with…”. It can remain unfinished. There is no requirement to end feeling peaceful.

**Cautions:** Do not weaponise lament to demand disclosure in groups. Participants should be able to pass, keep entries private, or stop. When trauma, abuse or severe distress is involved, prayer should accompany—not replace—safety, appropriate care, relationships and professional help. Psalm 88 itself resists a forced happy ending.

## 3. Petition — ask for what you need

**Meaning.** Petition is asking God for oneself: daily provision, wisdom, courage, healing, forgiveness, strength, a changed heart, or other practical needs. Luke 11 presents a persistent midnight request and a loving Father, concluding with the Father's gift of the Holy Spirit.

**Biblical anchors.** Luke 11:5–13; Matthew 6:11–13; Philippians 4:6–7; James 1:5; Mark 14:36.

**Notice:** What do I actually need today? What is within my responsibility, what cannot I control, and what can I ask God to do? Am I willing to ask directly, rather than hint?

**Original sample prayer:** “Father, please give me wisdom for this decision, courage to do what is right and your Holy Spirit to guide me. Help me receive the help I need.”

**Practice (1–3 minutes):** Speak one request clearly. If needed, write it as a simple sentence. Ask with persistence and openness, then consider one wise next action that does not depend on a miraculous outcome.

**Cautions:** Petition is not a vending-machine formula or a way of judging somebody's faith. Not every request is granted in the form or time requested. Saying “in Jesus' name” is not a magical suffix: the Practicing the Way Bible.com sample connects it to our standing in Christ and alignment with his life and purposes.

## 4. Intercession — ask on behalf of someone else

**Meaning.** Intercession is asking God to act for other people, groups, situations and the world, while leaving them their dignity and agency. It expands prayer beyond our own concerns and may shape how we love and serve others.

**Biblical anchors.** 1 Timothy 2:1–2; Ephesians 6:18; James 5:16; Exodus 32:11–14; Luke 11:5–13; Matthew 5:44.

**Notice:** Who needs provision, relief, healing, safety, reconciliation, justice or hope? Can I pray for them *without* assuming I know all their needs? What practical care can I offer without overstepping?

**Original sample prayer:** “God, please be near to those who are frightened, sick or exhausted. Give wisdom and strength to those caring for them. Show me one loving action I can take.”

**Practice (1–3 minutes):** Name a person, family, community or public situation. Ask specifically and kindly. Consider sending an encouraging message or offering practical help, **without treating prayer as a substitute for consent, boundaries or practical support**.

**Cautions:** Never enter another person's confidential health, relationship or prayer needs in a public GitHub repository, shared prayer list or analytics system. Avoid announcing that God has guaranteed a particular outcome or telling a sufferer their pain means they did not pray enough.

## Putting them together without making them a checklist

You can choose **one** mode or combine several. A simple invitation—not a sequence attributed to Comer:

1. **Arrive (30 seconds).** Notice your feet, shoulders or breathing. Become aware that you are before God.
2. **Choose freely (1–5 minutes).** Thank; lament; ask for yourself; or ask for others. Some days pain needs to be named before gratitude is possible.
3. **Leave space (30 seconds).** Be silent if helpful. Do not treat your next thought as an automatically infallible message from God.
4. **Respond.** If a responsible, loving action becomes clear, consider it. It is also all right simply to have prayed.

### Examples by situation

- **A difficult workday:** “Thank you for the colleague who helped.” Or “God, I feel overwhelmed.” Then ask for patience and wisdom; pray for colleagues.
- **Long-term unanswered prayer:** Lament first; use Psalm 13 or 88. Petition again if you want to. Avoid interpreting silence as personal failure.
- **A family meal:** Each willing person can offer one thanks, one need, or a prayer for someone else. Let children opt out; keep it short and unpressured.
- **When distracted or numb:** Use a short written prayer from Scripture. The *talking to God* session exists precisely because borrowed words can carry us when our own fail.

### A gentle seven-day experiment (optional, no streaks)

- Day 1: read Luke 11:1–13; say an honest one-sentence prayer.
- Day 2: notice something specific to thank God for.
- Day 3: pray Psalm 13 or 88 and name a grief.
- Day 4: ask directly for a real personal need.
- Day 5: intercede for someone, and consider a practical kindness.
- Day 6: choose whichever prayer best fits; avoid aiming for “balance”.
- Day 7: reflect on whether there was more honesty, connection or openness, not whether prayers were “successful”.

**Important nuance:** No promised psychological effect, guaranteed healing, or mechanical link between number of repetitions and answers. FaithHub intentionally has **no streak, grade, timer, public prayer-sharing or guilt engine**.

## How this sits in the wider four-movement teaching

- **Talking to God:** received language, such as the Lord's Prayer or Psalms, can support prayer when we lack words.
- **Talking with God:** the four categories explored here, as an accessible separation of gratitude, lament, petition and intercession.
- **Listening to God:** quiet and Scripture-attentive listening (including Lectio Divina) are further practices, not equivalent to automatically trusting any inner impulse.
- **Being with God:** contemplative attention to God's presence beyond constant speaking.

These movements can overlap; they are **not a hierarchy of spiritual superiority**, nor a test to complete in order. The end goal is loving relationship with God and transformation in Christ.
