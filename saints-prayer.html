A small school of prayer

Jesus, teach me to pray.

Prayer isn't a performance or a technique for manufacturing a feeling. It is turning honestly towards the living God. Begin with Jesus, listen to Scripture, speak as you are, and return when your mind wanders.

Rooted in Jesus' teaching (Matthew 6:5–13; Luke 11:1–13). Historic Catholic, Orthodox and other Christian voices are included for learning; no tradition's additional devotional practices are required.

A five-minute prayer to Jesus

Use the words below or your own. You can sit, stand or walk. You don't need to close your eyes or feel anything special. The timings are invitations, not targets.

  1. Arrive · 30 secondsFeel your feet on the ground. Let your hands rest. Say: “Jesus, I am here. You know me and love me.” Breathe normally; you don't need to control your breathing.
  2. Be honest · 1 minuteTell Jesus what is actually happening: joy, exhaustion, gratitude, fear, frustration, or numbness. Nothing needs tidying up.
  3. Listen to Scripture · 1 minuteRead Matthew 11:28–30. Notice what Jesus says about coming to him when weary. Ask: “Lord, what is there for me to receive?” Avoid pretending a thought is definitely God's voice.
  4. Ask and be still · 1½ minutesSay: “Jesus, help me trust you. Give me wisdom and patience. Please care for the people on my heart.” Sit quietly. If your attention moves, return gently. Silence is still prayer.
  5. Respond in love · 1 minutePray: “Lord Jesus, make me more loving, gentle and faithful. Show me one good thing to do next.” Choose a small ordinary act; don't measure your holiness by it.

When words run out: The ancient Jesus Prayer is “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner.” You can also simply pray “Jesus, have mercy” or “Jesus, be with me.” This is a prayer to Christ, not a magic formula. Read its Orthodox explanation.

When you want a framework, Jesus' Lord's Prayer teaches worship, surrender, daily needs, forgiveness and protection. Christians also pray to the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit (Ephesians 2:18); speaking directly to Jesus is a longstanding Christian practice.

What the saints taught about prayer

These are concise paraphrases of their writings, not invented quotations. Open each primary text to read the teaching in context.

St Augustine of Hippo

354–430 · Letter 130 to Proba, sections 17–20

Prayer stretches and forms our desire for God. Brief prayers throughout the day are valuable; honesty and longing matter more than many words. He also makes room for tears and wordless groaning.

Try: “Jesus, teach me to desire what is good.”

Read Augustine's letter

St Teresa of Ávila

1515–1582 · The Way of Perfection, chapter 26

Approach prayer in the company of Jesus himself: he is the teacher and friend. Teresa encouraged beginners to speak with him and consider his loving presence, rather than pursue elaborate techniques.

Try: “Jesus, help me talk with you as a friend.”

Read Teresa's chapter

St Ignatius of Loyola

1491–1556 · Spiritual Exercises, sections 53–54

Ignatius calls the personal conversation of prayer a colloquy: speak to Christ as a friend to a friend. Bring requests, your choices and your real life; finish with the Our Father if helpful.

Try: “Jesus, this is where I am. What would love look like?”

Read the Spiritual Exercises

Brother Lawrence

c. 1614–1691 · Practice of the Presence of God, fourth conversation and eighth letter

Ordinary work can become a place of conversation with God. When distracted, return without self-reproach. Offer a small task to God before you start and give thanks when you finish.

Try: “Jesus, help me do this ordinary thing with love.”

Read Brother Lawrence

St Benedict of Nursia

c. 480–547 · Rule, chapter 20

Prayer is not improved by the number of words. He commends humble, sincere and ordinarily brief prayer, while allowing longer prayer when grace invites it.

Try: one unhurried sentence to Jesus.

Read Benedict's Rule

St Thérèse of Lisieux

1873–1897 · Story of a Soul, chapter 11

Thérèse describes prayer as a movement of the heart that can hold gratitude or sorrow. She speaks to the Lord simply, like a child, without hunting for perfect phrases.

Try: “Jesus, here is what I want you to know.”
